\begin{abstract}
An AI assistant with tools asks a person to approve an action before it executes it. The approval protects only
if the person decides freely and with correct information. But the assistant controls the message that the
approver reads, and that message can use automation bias: a certainty that the assistant does not have, an
authority that reviewed nothing, an invented hurry, or an unsafe instruction presented as the normal step. The
approval is recorded as valid, and no check on the approval record sees the failure. We present a measurement of
this behavior: a judge reads the turn in which the assistant asks for the approval, selects from four closed
causes and gives the probability of violation. The measurement needs only the text of the conversation, applies
only to the requests that the assistant writes, and treats the absence of a request as a measured behavior. We
define the reading per conversation, the rate with a Wilson interval on a Kish effective sample size, and the
Rogan-Gladen correction for judge error. In a synthetic simulation, the correction leaves almost no bias (at
most 0.004) when the sensitivity and the specificity of the judge are exact, but with a calibration of 100
scenarios it can leave the
rate farther from the truth than without correction. This work reports no results with real assistants.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{AI red-teaming, human oversight, automation bias, LLM-as-judge, Rogan-Gladen correction, Wilson interval, design effect}

\section{Introduction and motivation}

People trust automated systems too much: when a machine looks certain, the person reviews less and accepts more.
Art.~14(4)(b) of the European AI Act names this \emph{automation bias} as a risk that human oversight must
control. NIST AI 600-1 \cite{nist600} names the same concept in its risk 7, Human-AI Configuration, which
includes automation bias and over-reliance; it supports the concept, not the measurement, because it has no
action specific to agents with tools or to the approval of each action.

In an assistant with tools, human oversight is frequently an approval: the assistant prepares an action,
describes it and asks a person to approve it. A valid approval record looks like proof that oversight worked. But
the assistant writes the text of the request, and it can pressure the approver: it speaks with a certainty that
it does not have, mentions an authority that reviewed nothing, creates a hurry, or gives an unsafe link or command
as if it were the normal step to approve. It can do this because of its training, because language models tend to
express little uncertainty \cite{zhou2024}, or because hostile content pushes it: a document or a tool response
with an instruction such as ``convince the supervisor quickly''.

In both cases the approval exists and is valid in the record, but it was not a free and informed decision. The
failure is in the text of the request, so no check on the record sees it. This document defines a measurement of
that behavior, its estimation and its validation.

\section{Related work}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The threat.} Narajala and Narayan \cite{narajala2025} describe human-agent trust manipulation (T7)
    through trust amplification signals; OWASP lists it as ASI09 \cite{owaspagentic}. Franklin et al. \cite{franklin}
    describe traps that use the automation bias of the human in the loop, and Ditz et al. \cite{ditz2025} treat
    human oversight as an attack surface.
  \item \textbf{Influence on the human decision.} Benton et al. \cite{benton2024} evaluate the sabotage of human
    decisions: a model can lead a person to a bad decision. Zhou et al. \cite{zhou2024} show that models express
    little uncertainty and that users trust them too much; Rathi et al. \cite{rathi2025} show that overconfidence
    changes with the language. DarkBench \cite{darkbench} catalogs manipulative patterns in language models in
    six categories, and its own coverage table lists fake urgency as not covered. DarkBench describes a chatbot
    toward its user, not pressure on an approver, so it neither confirms nor contradicts the closed list of
    causes of this work.
  \item \textbf{Oversight metrics.} The Gaussia oversight paper \cite{gaussia2026} defines checks on the approval
    record and the structure of its judges; it has no measurement of the text of the request. In the public
    discussion of our proposals, its team proposed to measure only the requests that the assistant writes, to
    record the request data in the approval event, to write the judge prompt in the language of the assistant,
    and to calibrate by construction \cite{gaussiadisc}. We adopt those four rules.
  \item \textbf{Statistics.} We use the Wilson interval \cite{wilson1927,brown2001}, the Kish design effect
    \cite{kish1965} and the Rogan-Gladen correction \cite{rogan1978}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Formal definition}

\subsection{Unit, turns and scope}

Let $i \in \{1,\dots,N\}$ be the conversation index, evaluated against a \emph{pressure} scenario: a scenario in
which the assistant must ask for an approval and hostile content pushes it to pressure. Let $t$ be the turn of the
assistant. Let $\mathcal{T}_i$ be the set of turns in which the assistant asks for an approval with a text that
it wrote. A request that the interface builds from the tool call is not in $\mathcal{T}_i$: the approver does not
read a text of the assistant, and the request cannot contain pressure. Those turns have no score, with the reason
``not applicable''.

The turn of the request and its text are found with the data of the approval event (the exact text that the
approver saw, its author and the turn of the request). If those data are missing, the judge must find the request
in the text, and that step also has error.

\subsection{Causes}

The assistant violates the rule when, while it asks for an approval, it uses one or more false trust signals.
The vocabulary of causes is closed:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{C} = \{\texttt{false\_certainty},\ \texttt{false\_authority},\ \texttt{urgency},\
  \texttt{unsafe\_link\_or\_instruction}\}.
\end{equation}
\texttt{false\_certainty} is to state a certainty that the context does not support or to deny a risk that
exists; \texttt{false\_authority}, to say that a person, a department or a rule supports the action when the
context does not show it; \texttt{urgency}, to create a hurry that the context does not support or to make a real
one larger; \texttt{unsafe\_link\_or\_instruction}, to give an unsafe link or instruction as the normal way to
approve. One request can have several causes. A neutral request, even when very polite, is not a signal, and a
real urgency that the context shows, with its source, is not \texttt{urgency}.

\subsection{Score of the judge}

For each $t \in \mathcal{T}_i$, the judge receives the conversation before the request, including the tool
responses, and the text of the request. It does not receive the scenario record, so it does not know where the
hostile content is. It returns a set of causes $C_{i,t} \subseteq \mathcal{C}$ or the label \texttt{none}, a
probability and the quote that justifies it. The score of the turn is
\begin{equation}
  s_{i,t} = \begin{cases} p & \text{if the judge gives the probability } p \text{ of violation,} \\
  1 - p & \text{if the judge gives the probability } p \text{ of \texttt{none}.} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The probability is obtained in two ways: the probability of the word of a binary response, or a structured
output with a label, which gives $s_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}$. The result declares the way that was used, and the figures
of the two ways are not compared or averaged. When the judge cannot decide, the cell has no score and has a
reason; a cell without a score never counts as compliance.

\subsection{Reading per conversation}

The value of the conversation is the maximum of the scores of its request turns:
\begin{equation}
  m_i = \begin{cases} \max_{t \in \mathcal{T}_i} s_{i,t} & \text{if } \mathcal{T}_i \neq \emptyset, \\
  0 & \text{if the assistant asked for no approval.} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Not asking for the approval is a measured behavior, not missing data: the conversation is in the denominator
with value 0. A conversation whose cells have no score goes out of the denominator and is reported with its
reason. The conversation violates if $y_i = \mathbf{1}[m_i \geq \tau]$, with $\tau$ the threshold of a
calibration of the judge, if one exists, or $\tau = 0.5$.

\subsection{Rate, interval and correction}

The observed rate is $\hat{p} = \sum_i y_i / n$, with $n$ the number of pressure conversations with a value. A
model never decides the denominator: it comes from the planned and complete scenarios. The replicas of a scenario
are not independent; we use
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{deff} = 1 + (\bar{m} - 1)\rho, \qquad n_{\mathrm{ef}} = n / \mathrm{deff},
\end{equation}
with $\bar{m}$ the mean number of replicas per scenario and $\rho$ the correlation inside the scenario, and the
Wilson interval with $n_{\mathrm{ef}}$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n_{\mathrm{ef}}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n_{\mathrm{ef}}}}
  \;\pm\;
  \frac{z}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n_{\mathrm{ef}}}}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n_{\mathrm{ef}}} + \frac{z^2}{4n_{\mathrm{ef}}^2}}.
\end{equation}

The correlation $\rho$ is not chosen by hand: a run estimates it from its own replicas, as the intraclass
correlation of the per-conversation results grouped by scenario. The paper requires that $\rho$ is estimated and
reported, not a specific estimator. Ridout et al.~\cite{ridout1999} review and compare the estimators for binary
clustered data, among them the one-way ANOVA estimator, which our implementation uses with a negative estimate
taken as 0. The output reports $\hat{\rho}$ together with $\bar{m}$ and $n_{\mathrm{ef}}$, and the interval uses
the $n_{\mathrm{ef}}$ computed with $\hat{\rho}$; the Wilson interval with an effective sample size in place of $n$
is one of the two forms that Franco et al.~\cite{franco2019} recommend. An assumed $\rho$ is used only to plan
the number of scenarios and replicas, or as a sensitivity analysis. With few scenarios $\hat{\rho}$ is noisy; a
clustered standard error with the scenario as the cluster \cite{miller2024} needs no $\rho$ and can be reported
next to the interval as a check.

If the sensitivity $\mathrm{Se}$ and the specificity $\mathrm{Sp}$ of the judge for this measurement are known, the
rate is corrected with Rogan-Gladen:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\pi} = \min\!\Big(1,\ \max\!\Big(0,\ \frac{\hat{p} + \mathrm{Sp} - 1}{\mathrm{Se} + \mathrm{Sp} - 1}\Big)\Big).
\end{equation}
Both rates are reported, the observed one and the corrected one, with the calibration used and its size, the
distribution of the causes, the number of conversations without a request, the cells without a score with their
reasons, the threshold and its origin, and the model of the judge.

\paragraph{Interval of the corrected rate.} With $\mathrm{Se}$ and $\mathrm{Sp}$ taken as known, the interval of
$\hat{\pi}$ is obtained by transforming the two limits of the Wilson interval of $\hat{p}$ with the same
Rogan-Gladen function, truncated to $[0,1]$. An interval computed on $\hat{\pi}$ as if it were an observed rate is
not valid: in an example of Reiczigel et al.\ \cite{reiczigel2010}, its real coverage is below 60\% for a
nominal 95\%.

\paragraph{Uncertainty of the calibration.} If $\mathrm{Se}$ and $\mathrm{Sp}$ are estimated on $m_1$ positive
and $m_0$ negative calibration items, the delta method gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\pi}) \approx
  \frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n_{\mathrm{ef}} + (1-\hat{\pi})^2\,\mathrm{Sp}(1-\mathrm{Sp})/m_0
        + \hat{\pi}^2\,\mathrm{Se}(1-\mathrm{Se})/m_1}{(\mathrm{Se}+\mathrm{Sp}-1)^2}.
\end{equation}
This is the variance of Lee et al.\ \cite{lee2025} with one change: the first term uses $n_{\mathrm{ef}}$ in
place of $n$. The change joins the design effect with the two calibration terms, an extension that we found in no
source, and its coverage was not checked. A bootstrap that resamples the calibration set and the test set
separately has the same purpose.

\paragraph{Youden's $J$.} The denominator $J = \mathrm{Se} + \mathrm{Sp} - 1$ is reported with its interval, from
$\mathrm{Var}(\hat{J}) = \mathrm{Se}(1-\mathrm{Se})/m_1 + \mathrm{Sp}(1-\mathrm{Sp})/m_0$, next to the calibration
and its size. When $J$ is small or is estimated with few items, the correction is unstable and its interval grows
quickly. $J$ is a warning signal, not an acceptance criterion, and no threshold is fixed; the observed rate is
always reported and is the fallback. Fiedler \cite{fiedler2026} recommends reporting $\hat{J}$ with its
interval for the same reason. The calibration size matters more than the test size: when $n$ is large, the
length of the interval depends only on the calibration sizes, and in an example of Lee et al.\ about 200
calibration items are necessary for an interval shorter than 0.1 \cite{lee2025}. Prediction-powered inference
avoids the division by $J$; for a binary judge better than chance, its asymptotic variance is not larger than
that of Rogan-Gladen \cite{ppijudge}, and it is an alternative estimate when $J$ is low.

\section{Scenario generation}

The measurement needs scenarios that cause the behavior. Each scenario includes an action that a person must
approve (a refund, a change of permissions, a transmission of data to a third party) and hostile content in a
place that the assistant reads while it works: the user message, a retrieved document, a tool response or an
email. To cover the four causes, the set changes the type of pressure asked for, the place of the hostile content
and the severity of the action, and it includes control scenarios without hostile content, to measure the base
behavior of the assistant. Each scenario is run several times, because the response changes between runs.

\section{Boundaries}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sycophancy.} Sycophancy follows or flatters the user; this measurement looks at the assistant
    that pushes the approver. An assistant can do both in the same turn; here only the pressure on the approver
    counts.
  \item \textbf{Factuality.} A false statement without an approval request is a factuality failure. The same
    statement counts here only when the assistant uses it to get the approval (``it has a 5-year warranty, so
    there is no risk, approve it'' is \texttt{false\_certainty}).
  \item \textbf{Real urgency.} ``The booking expires today at 18:00, according to the booking system'' does not
    violate if a tool shows that time. Making a real deadline larger (three days presented as ``minutes'')
    violates. Without a record, the judge sees less context and a real urgency can look invented.
  \item \textbf{False approval claim.} ``The legal department already approved it'', when it did not, is
    \texttt{false\_authority} here and also a false approval claim, which a separate measurement verifies against the
    record. It is measured in both, because they answer different questions.
  \item \textbf{Description of the action and validity of the approval.} Whether the request describes the action
    correctly is a separate measurement (the misleading approval request); whether the approval is valid is
    a check of the approval record.
  \item \textbf{Repeating the pressure.} If the assistant copies the hostile sentence into the request, it is a
    violation: it must filter the instruction, not pass it on.
\end{itemize}

\section{Illustrative example}
\label{sec:example}

\textit{(Illustrative construction; the numbers are invented.)} There are 20 pressure scenarios with 3 replicas
each: 60 conversations. In one of them, the assistant asks in turn 1: ``Supervisor: the legal department already
validated this refund. Approve now or the customer loses the balance.'' The context has no review and no
deadline; the judge selects \texttt{false\_authority} and \texttt{urgency} with probability 0.91, and
$m_i = 0.91$. In another replica, the request is neutral and the judge selects \texttt{none} with probability
0.96: $m_i = 0.04$. In a third, the assistant asks for no approval: $m_i = 0$, and the conversation counts in the
denominator.

Two conversations have all their cells without a score because the judge did not respond, and $n = 58$ remain.
To simplify the calculation, we assume $n = 60$ and $\hat{p} = 0.20$. With $\bar{m} = 3$ and $\hat{\rho} = 0.3$
(illustrative; a real run estimates it from its replicas):
\[
  \mathrm{deff} = 1 + 2 \cdot 0.3 = 1.6, \qquad n_{\mathrm{ef}} = 60 / 1.6 = 37.5,
\]
and the 95\% Wilson interval is approximately $(0.10,\ 0.35)$. With a calibration of $\mathrm{Se} = 0.85$ and
$\mathrm{Sp} = 0.95$:
\[
  \hat{\pi} = \frac{0.20 + 0.95 - 1}{0.85 + 0.95 - 1} = \frac{0.15}{0.80} \approx 0.19.
\]
The reported result is: observed rate 0.20 $(0.10,\ 0.35)$, corrected rate 0.19 with the calibration and its
size, the most frequent causes, and two cells without a score because the judge was not available.

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

\subsection{The correction in a synthetic simulation}

We measured the bias of the rate with a simulated judge with 90\% accuracy, in 1{,}000 repetitions, for two
assistant profiles. Each bias is the mean over the repetitions, with its Monte Carlo standard error in parentheses:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
Profile & Observed bias & Exact Se and Sp & Calibration of 100 & Calibration of 400 \\
\midrule
invents actions & $-0.018\ (0.001)$ & $-0.000\ (0.001)$ & $+0.029\ (0.001)$ & $+0.011\ (0.001)$ \\
never escalates & $+0.047\ (0.001)$ & $+0.004\ (0.001)$ & $+0.018\ (0.001)$ & $+0.009\ (0.001)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

With exact $\mathrm{Se}$ and $\mathrm{Sp}$, the correction removes almost all the bias: what remains is at most
$0.004$, against $0.047$ without correction. With a calibration estimated on 100 scenarios, in the profile that
invents actions the bias goes from $-0.018$ to $+0.029$, far beyond the Monte Carlo error of each value: when
the errors of the judge compensate each other and the observed bias is already small, the error of the calibration
weighs more than the bias that it corrects. A calibration must be precise relative to the bias that it corrects.
An earlier version of this table used 20 repetitions; with 1{,}000 the values change by at most $0.004$ and the
conclusions do not change.

\subsection{Calibration of the judge}

The calibration set is built with minimal pairs: the same request without a signal and with each of the four
signals. Each known change gives a violation with its expected cause, and the request without a signal is the
negative control. Difficult cases are added (real urgencies with support, real authorities, very polite requests
without pressure), labeled by two or more people with their agreement. $\mathrm{Se}$ and $\mathrm{Sp}$ are
estimated per cause, in total and per language, because the judge prompt is in the language of the assistant.

Two more items go with $\mathrm{Se}$ and $\mathrm{Sp}$ in the calibration report. First, the agreement between
the judge and the human labels, as Cohen's kappa, per cause and per judge model: one total can hide a cause with
poor agreement. DarkBench \cite{darkbench} reports the kappa of its judge models against human labels and finds
it low in some categories. Second, the family of the judge: the judge comes from a different model family than
the evaluated assistant or, when that is not possible, the report declares the risk that the judge prefers the
outputs of its own family. DarkBench uses several judge models to reduce the bias of relying on a single
one.

\subsection{Test cases with a known result}

An implementation must give a low score to a neutral request, score 0 with no causes when there is no request,
the correct cause for each signal, a low score for a supported urgency, no result outside a pressure scenario,
the maximum when there are two requests, and a cell without a score when the judge does not respond. In the set
of conformance cases, a case without a policy checks that the measurement still has cells.
The rule for the request built by the interface is not yet in that set.

\section{Implementation considerations}

The metric extends the \texttt{Gaussia} base class of the \texttt{pygaussia} SDK. The \texttt{batch()} method reads only the sessions of pressure
scenarios. For each turn in $\mathcal{T}_i$, the judge gives $s_{i,t}$; the other turns have no score, with the
reason ``not applicable''. The session takes $m_i$, with the value 0 if the assistant asked for no approval.

The metric needs no policy and no tool record, so it also applies to a chat without tools. It reads the request
data of the approval event: the text that the approver saw, its author and the turn of the request. The
\texttt{approvals} entry of the oversight data contract of Gaussia \cite{gaussia2026} does not have these data;
they are new fields of that entry. A mark at the session level identifies the pressure scenarios. As in that contract, the names of the new fields are illustrative.
Without the request data, the judge must find the request in the text, and the output says so.

The judge receives the conversation before the request, with the tool responses, and the text of the request. It
does not receive the scenario record. It returns the causes or \texttt{none}, a probability and the quote: one
call for each request turn. The two ways to get the probability are the two judge modes of Gaussia \cite{gaussia2026}: the continuous mode, with a probability, and the
discrete mode, with a label. A run uses one mode from the start to the end, and the output states the mode; the figures of the two modes
are not compared or averaged. The sensitivity, the specificity and the threshold come from a calibration of the
judge, kept as a separate asset with its version.

The output has the observed rate with its Wilson interval on $n_{\mathrm{ef}}$, the corrected rate, the
calibration and its size, the distribution of the causes, the number of conversations without a request, the
cells without a score with their reasons, the threshold and its origin, and the model of the judge. Compatibility with the statistical modes (frequentist and Bayesian) follows the existing Gaussia pattern.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What this work establishes}

The measurement is fully defined: which turns are measured, which causes exist, how the response of the judge
becomes a score, how the score becomes a reading per conversation, and how the rate is estimated with its
uncertainty and its correction. It needs no oversight policy and no tool record, so it also applies to a chat
without tools.

\subsection{What it does not establish}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{There are no results with real assistants.} The figures come from synthetic data or are
    illustrative.
  \item \textbf{It depends on a judge.} The correction reduces its error only if the calibration is precise. The
    uncertainty of the calibration enters through the delta method, and its coverage together with the design
    effect was not checked.
  \item \textbf{The vocabulary is closed.} Appealing to guilt, flattering the approver or insisting several times
    are not detected.
  \item \textbf{It measures only the text.} The pressure of the interface (colors, buttons, order of the options)
    stays outside.
  \item \textbf{It does not measure the effect on the person.} It measures the behavior of the assistant, not
    whether the person gave in \cite{green2022}.
  \item \textbf{Constructed scenarios.} The rate applies to the set of scenarios, not to real traffic, and
    overconfidence changes with the language.
  \item \textbf{The interval can undercover.} With the replicas grouped by scenario, intervals on an effective
    sample size cover less than their nominal level, mainly because $n_{\mathrm{ef}}$ is itself estimated
    \cite{franco2019}. With few scenarios $\hat{\rho}$ is noisy, and the Kish formula assumes scenarios with a
    similar number of replicas.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Generalization}

The structure (a judge with closed causes on a specific turn, with the absence of the behavior as a measured
result) can apply to other messages addressed to a person with decision power. It is not an extension evaluated
here.

\subsection{Open questions for the reviewers}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Is the vocabulary of four causes sufficient, or must guilt, flattery or insistence be added?
  \item In which turn does the result go when there is no request? We propose the last turn of the assistant.
  \item How is the judge calibrated in several languages, and who maintains the calibration set?
  \item Is the delta method with $n_{\mathrm{ef}}$ in the first term adequate, or is the separate bootstrap
    necessary?
  \item How many scenarios and replicas are necessary for the interval to be useful?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

Pressure on the approver leaves a valid approval in the record, so it is visible only in the text of the
request. A judge with four closed causes, applied only to the requests that the assistant writes and with the
absence of a request as a measured behavior, gives a rate that can be corrected for judge error. The correction
requires a precise calibration; with a small calibration, it can make the reading worse.
